What the audit found

The upstream ledger is a genuinely useful piece of research — 206 entries, honest about the difference between gems and dollars, and explicit that official rules win every argument. This audit did not find a scam. It found a dataset that has outgrown the rules it set for itself.

1. Six headline findings

  1. The headline count overstates what is on offer. "206 listings" is real, but 21 rows carry a non-cash prize, 28 state no figure at all, and 9 are not competitions in the first place — pool-hosting tools and directories. The number of rows with a genuine free path and a stated cash prize is materially smaller than 206.
  2. 12 rows break the project's own sourcing rule. The upstream README requires that any stated prize figure cite the operator's own page. These rows cite only affiliate, forum or social sources — and 163 rows overall are still labelled live, so some uncited figures are presented as current.
  3. A further 13 sources are mislabelled as official. They grade press-release wires, broker review aggregators, affiliate bonus blogs, club fan sites and X posts as the operator's own word. Three live rows quoting precise prize tables rest entirely on affiliate blogs — the exact failure mode the rule was written to prevent.
  4. The trading category is a category error. 19 rows are broker demo-account contests. These are customer-acquisition promotions; prizes are frequently credited as trading bonuses or prop accounts rather than payable cash. The upstream file excluded bookmaker and DFS funnels on precisely this logic but kept forex broker funnels.
  5. Status has decayed. 27 rows are marked ended, which is honest. But status is inherited rather than observed, and the sports category has 129 of the 206 rows, almost all seasonal. At least one row I verified — MLB Beat the Streak — is marked live although its own rules end the entry period with the 2026 regular season, which has finished.
  6. The strongest real opportunities are the least glamorous. Metaculus, DrivenData, ARC Prize and Kaggle reward measurable accuracy or model quality, are free to enter, state their prizes on their own pages, and have no rake. They also suit an AI coding agent far better than bracket-picking does.

4. Sourcing quality, measured

The upstream rule is strict and correct: any listing that states a specific prize figure must cite the operator's own rules or game page. Measured against it:

FindingRows
No official-tier source at all12
Source mislabelled official (aggregator, wire, club site, social)13
Official source exists but not on the operator's domain20
"Rules" link is the homepage35
No rules link at all6

Two patterns explain almost all of it. First, trading-broker rows — myfxbook.com press releases and affiliate bonus blogs standing in for the broker's own contest terms. Second, niche fantasy leagues — where the prize exists only inside a league's own app, so the compiler reached for a Reddit thread or an FPL tips blog.

The second pattern is the more forgivable: for a private mini-league there may genuinely be no public page to cite. The correct handling is to say so and leave the figure blank, which is what this site does. Six such rows now carry a blank prize and a NO_OFFICIAL_SOURCE flag instead of a number borrowed from a blog.

5. Category-by-category verdict

Tech & data — 14 rows. The strongest group.

Free entry, published rules, automatic scoring, real prize pools and no house rake. Kaggle, DrivenData, ARC Prize and Metaculus were all opened and confirmed during this audit. Prize sizes run from a few thousand dollars to the low millions, and the work is the kind of work an agent can do: read a spec, write code, iterate against a leaderboard.

Fantasy & sports picks — 129 rows. Large, free, seasonal.

The free-entry claim holds up well here: broadcaster and league pick'em games really are free and really do pay advertiser-funded cash. CBS Pro Pick'em was verified in detail — free entry, 18 weekly $1,000 prizes, a $5,000 season prize and a $100,000 perfect-week prize, restricted to the 50 states plus DC. The weakness is timing: nearly all of it is in-season only, and status is not refreshed.

Trading & market — 19 rows. The weakest group.

13 rows carry a broker-funnel flag and 14 rows across the site carry a cash-out restriction flag — bonus credit, prop accounts, non-withdrawable balances. Several "prizes" are funded trading accounts, which are a liability-shaped asset that requires further trading to realise. If the goal is cash for time spent, this category should be near the bottom of the queue regardless of the headline numbers, which are the largest on the site.

Skill tournaments — 12 rows. Mostly paid.

The upstream file is commendably blunt here: free rooms use gems, cash rooms charge a fee, and the platform takes a rake. Only a handful have a real free-to-cash path. This is the category where the word "free" most often means "free practice".

Creative — 18 rows. Judged, and uneven.

Genuine prizes exist (Louisiana Film Prize, HIPA, the Sony and World Press Photo awards) but outcomes depend on subjective judging. Two rows — 99designs and Freelancer.com — are client-funded marketplaces where the "prize" is a fee-deducted work payment. That is a job board, not a contest, and should be labelled as one.

Forecasting — 3 rows. Small and honest.

Metaculus is the standout on the whole site: its homepage states outright that it is not a prediction market and that you can "participate for free and win cash prizes", with 19 tournaments and named pools up to $50,000. Small field, clear rules, measurable skill.

Pool tools and directories — 7 + 2 rows. Not contests.

Seven pool hosts ("prizes decided by your group") and two directories. Useful, but listing them among competitions that pay prizes inflates the count and misleads a reader scanning for opportunities. They belong in an appendix.

6. Where an AI coding agent actually has an edge

The plan is to use a coding agent to compete in hard competitions for real money. That narrows the useful list sharply, because the binding constraint is not prize size — it is whether effort compounds and whether scoring is objective.

TestWhy it mattersCategories that pass
Objective, automated scoringAn agent can measure its own progress without human judging.Tech & data; forecasting

Verdict: concentrate on Tech & data and Forecasting, treat Fantasy & sports picks as low-effort, low-ceiling entertainment with a small number of genuinely free prizes, and deprioritise Trading and Skill, where the fee, the rake or the credit terms eat the expected value.

The honest caveat on prize size. Kaggle's Featured and Hackathon tracks pay well but attract thousands of teams; an agent raises throughput, not the odds to first place. The competitions with the best ratio of prize to competition are usually the smaller ones — a $3,400 forecasting pool with a few hundred entrants is a better use of an afternoon than a $450,000 track with 371 teams. Sort by prize, then check the entrant count on the operator's page before committing.

7. Status decay and seasonality

Status is copied from the upstream snapshot, which was reviewed 2026-08-19, and it is not re-polled. That produces three predictable distortions:

  • Seasonal contests read as live out of season. MLB Beat the Streak is marked live, but its own 2026 rules close the entry period at the end of the regular season — which has finished. Its prize is also conditional to the point of being illustrative: $5,600,000 only for a 57-game hitting streak, otherwise $10,000, and nothing at all if nobody reaches 20.
  • Annual contests that already ran. 27 rows are correctly marked ended and kept deliberately, which is good practice — they show what returns next season.
  • "Caution" is doing quiet work. 11 rows sit at caution, several because their own prize field says "not confirmed for 2026/27". That is the most honest thing in the dataset and it is why those prizes are blank on this site.

8. Patterns to avoid, and why

Nothing in this dataset looked like outright fraud. The risks are structural, and they are worth naming because they cost time rather than money:

  • Bonus credit dressed as cash. If the prize is "bonus cash", "trading credit" or "prop account", it is not money until further conditions are met. 14 rows carry this flag.
  • Entry fees with a rake. A free practice mode plus a raked cash mode means the free path never pays. Four rows require a fee outright and 13 more only offer free practice.
  • Conditional headline prizes. A $5,600,000 headline that requires a near-math impossible streak is marketing, not an expected value. Read the conditions, not the number.
  • Prize-figure laundering through affiliate pages. The upstream project found that 9 of 12 confirmed errors traced to affiliate sources. The remaining mislabels on this site are almost all of that type.
  • Any competition that asks you to pay a fee to collect a prize is illegitimate. The upstream file says this plainly and it remains the single best filter.

9. Recommendations

  1. Split the ledger by what is actually paid. Cash, mixed, non-cash and unstated should be separate views, not one count. This site does that.
  2. Move pool tools and directories to an appendix. They are useful, but they are not competitions and they inflate the headline number.
  3. Enforce the tier rule mechanically. A source can only be official if its registrable domain matches the operator's. That single check catches 13 of the current mislabels automatically.
  4. Blank the figure when there is no operator page. 12 rows should show no number, and the three affiliate-only live rows should lose their graded prize tables until a broker page is cited.
  5. Re-verify status at display time, or drop the field. A stale "live" is worse than no status. Where status cannot be refreshed, show the review date next to it — which this site does on every row.
  6. Keep the honest labels. The free-path stamp, the deliberate retention of ended entries, and the refusal to dress up gems as dollars are the best features of the upstream project. They are carried over unchanged.
